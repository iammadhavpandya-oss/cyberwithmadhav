A Day in the Life of a SOC Analyst: What the Job Really Looks Like

The security operations centre (SOC) is one of the most common first jobs in cybersecurity. Here is a realistic picture of what the work feels like, without the movie drama.

Quick answer

A SOC analyst monitors security alerts from tools like SIEM and EDR, decides whether each alert is a real threat or a false positive (triage), investigates the real ones, escalates them with clear notes, and documents everything. L1 analysts focus on triage, L2 on deeper investigation, L3 and engineers on hunting and tuning. Many SOCs run 24x7 in shifts.

What is a SOC?

A security operations centre is a team, sometimes in a dedicated room, that watches an organisation's systems for attacks. Some companies run their own SOC. Many use a managed security service provider (MSSP), where one SOC monitors many client companies. India has many such SOCs, which is why SOC roles are a common entry point for freshers here.

Typical tools include a SIEM (collects and correlates logs), EDR (watches laptops and servers), email security tools, firewalls and a ticketing system to track cases.

Start of the shift

Many SOCs run around the clock, so analysts often work rotating shifts, including nights and weekends. A shift usually starts with a handover: the previous shift explains open cases, ongoing incidents and anything to watch. Then you check the dashboard, the queue of new alerts, and any advisories that came in, such as a new critical vulnerability.

Alert triage: the core of the job

Most of the day is triage. An alert comes in, for example "multiple failed logins followed by a success from a new country". You ask:

  1. What exactly triggered this? Read the rule and the raw log.
  2. Is it expected? Maybe the user is travelling, or it is a known testing tool.
  3. What else happened around it? Check the user's other logins, the device, the IP reputation.
  4. Verdict: false positive, benign but notable, or true positive.

A true positive is escalated with clear notes. A false positive is closed with a reason, and sometimes the rule is tuned so it does not fire again for the same harmless case. Speed matters, but accuracy matters more.

Alerts you will see often

  • Phishing emails reported by staff.
  • Suspicious logins: impossible travel, many failures, logins at strange hours.
  • Malware detections on laptops.
  • Unusual network traffic, like a laptop talking to a known bad domain.
  • Policy violations, such as USB storage use where it is not allowed.

The MITRE ATT&CK knowledge base is a common reference to map what an attacker is doing, such as initial access, persistence or exfiltration.

Escalation and reporting

When something is real, L1 escalates to L2 or the incident response team with a good summary: what happened, when, which user and device, evidence and actions taken. Good writing saves time during an incident.

In India, organisations also have legal reporting duties. CERT-In's directions issued in April 2022 under Section 70B of the IT Act require specified types of cyber incidents to be reported to CERT-In within 6 hours of noticing them. SOC teams help their organisations meet this.

Skills you need for your first SOC role

  • Networking basics: IPs, ports, DNS, common protocols (Parts 3 and 5).
  • Windows event logs and Linux logs.
  • Phishing email analysis: headers, links and attachments.
  • Basic understanding of malware types and attacker techniques.
  • Comfort with search queries in a SIEM. Many SIEMs have free training editions.
  • Calm communication and clear writing.

The good and the hard parts

The good: you see real attacks, learn fast and build a strong base for any future security role. The hard: shifts, repetitive alerts and occasional pressure during incidents. Many analysts move to L2, incident response, threat hunting, detection engineering or cloud security after one or two years. If you enjoy puzzles and patterns, SOC work can be very satisfying.

Frequently asked questions

Do SOC analysts need to code? Not much at L1. Being able to read and tweak search queries in a SIEM is more important at the start. Scripting in Python or PowerShell helps a lot as you grow.

Is night shift compulsory? It depends on the company. Many SOCs that offer 24x7 monitoring use rotating shifts. Ask about the shift pattern during the interview so there are no surprises.

What should I practise to get a SOC job? Analyse phishing emails, read Windows and Linux logs in your lab, learn the basics of one SIEM through free training editions, and write clear incident style notes for everything you investigate.

Key takeaways

  • SOC work is alert triage, investigation, escalation and documentation.
  • Tools vary, but SIEM, EDR and ticketing systems are common everywhere.
  • Strong basics in networking, Windows, Linux and phishing analysis matter most.
  • Clear notes and calm thinking are as important as technical skill.
