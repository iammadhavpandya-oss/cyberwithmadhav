Passkeys in 2026: What They Are and Why Big Apps Are Switching

Passkeys let you sign in with your fingerprint, face or phone screen lock instead of a password. In 2026 they moved from a niche feature to a normal way to log in. Here is what they are and whether you should switch.

Quick answer

A passkey is a login credential stored on your device or in your password manager that uses public key cryptography. You unlock it with your fingerprint, face or screen lock. Because it is tied to the real website, a fake site cannot use it, so passkeys resist phishing. The FIDO Alliance's Passkey Index 2026 reports that 36% of successful sign-ins at participating services used a passkey, up from 26% in its 2025 index.

What exactly is a passkey?

When you create a passkey for a website, your device creates a pair of keys. The private key stays on your device or in your password manager. The public key goes to the website. When you log in, the website sends a challenge, your device signs it with the private key after you unlock it with your fingerprint, face or PIN, and the site checks the signature with the public key.

You never type a secret, the website never stores a password, and there is nothing useful for a database breach to leak.

Why passkeys stop phishing

With a password or OTP, you can be tricked into typing it on a fake page like amaz0n-offers.example. A passkey is bound to the real website's domain. If you are on a fake site, your device simply will not offer the passkey for it. The scammer has nothing to steal.

This is why security teams are excited. The FIDO Alliance's Passkey Index 2026 says every participant that measures phishing reported fewer successful attacks since deploying passkeys.

What the 2026 data says

The FIDO Alliance, the industry body behind passkey standards, published two reports in 2026:

  • State of Passkeys 2026 (released 7 May 2026), based on surveys of 11,000 consumers and 1,400 enterprise decision makers across ten countries including India. It reported that 90% of people surveyed are aware of passkeys, 75% have enabled a passkey on at least one account, and the Alliance estimated about 5 billion passkeys in use worldwide.
  • Passkey Index 2026, based on data from 15 large consumer services, compiled on 21 September 2026. It reported that 90% of active accounts at participants were eligible for passkeys, 42% of eligible accounts had one enrolled, and 36% of successful sign-ins used a passkey, compared with 26% in the 2025 index. Passkey sign-ins succeeded more often than traditional MFA sign-ins (93% versus 78%).

The Index notes that because the participant group changed from 9 to 15, comparisons with last year are directional. Still, the trend is clear: passkeys are becoming normal.

How to set up a passkey

The steps vary by service, but generally:

  1. Open the account's Security or Sign-in settings.
  2. Choose Create a passkey or Add passkey.
  3. Confirm with your fingerprint, face or screen lock.
  4. Your passkey is saved to your phone's account (such as Google Password Manager or iCloud Keychain) or your password manager, and can sync to your other devices.

Start with your main email account, then shopping and social media apps that offer it.

Common concerns

  • What if I lose my phone? If your passkeys sync through your Google, Apple or password manager account, they come back when you sign in on a new device. Keep a recovery method such as backup codes or a second device.
  • Does the website get my fingerprint? No. Your biometric only unlocks the key on your device. It is not sent to the website.
  • Can someone with my phone log in? They would also need to unlock it, which is why a strong screen lock is essential.
  • Shared family phones: be careful, because anyone who can unlock the device may be able to use the passkeys on it.

Do I still need passwords?

For now, yes, for many sites. Many services still keep a password as a fallback. The Passkey Index says two thirds of participants plan to offer passwordless accounts in the next 12 months. Until then, keep using a password manager with unique passwords, and use passkeys wherever available. Read our strong passwords guide.

Why this matters in India

Indians face constant phishing by SMS and WhatsApp, and OTP theft is a common scam pattern. Passkeys remove both the password and the OTP from the login, which takes away much of what scammers try to steal. When your apps offer passkeys, turning them on is one of the easiest upgrades you can make. "The stress of remembering passwords goes away, and the fear of phishing drops too."

Key takeaways

  • Passkeys replace passwords with a cryptographic key unlocked by your fingerprint, face or PIN.
  • They resist phishing because they only work on the genuine website.
  • FIDO Alliance data shows passkeys becoming a routine way to sign in during 2026.
  • Turn them on for important accounts, but keep a recovery method ready.
