Kids Online Safety: A Practical Guide for Indian Parents

Children today grow up online, for school, games and friends. Blocking everything rarely works. Open conversations, sensible settings and knowing where to report problems work much better.

By Madhav Pandya · Published 15 November 2026 · Last reviewed 15 November 2026 · 4 min read

kidsparentinggamingsocial media

Quick answer

Talk early and often, set up parental controls (Google Family Link or Apple Screen Time), keep devices in shared spaces for younger kids, review game and social media privacy settings, teach them never to share personal details or photos with online strangers, and make sure they know they can tell you anything without being punished. Report child abuse material or exploitation at cybercrime.gov.in.

Start with conversation, not control

The best protection is a child who comes to you when something feels wrong. If kids fear that telling you will mean losing their phone forever, they will hide problems. Tell them clearly: "Kuch bhi ajeeb ho, mujhe batana. Daant nahi padegi." Then keep that promise.

Talk about online life the way you talk about school: which games, which friends, what videos they enjoyed. Curiosity, not interrogation.

Set up controls by age

  • Under 10: use devices in shared spaces, with a child profile. Approve app downloads. Use kids versions of video apps where available.
  • 10 to 13: parental controls with app approval and screen time limits. Explain why each rule exists.
  • 13 and above: shift gradually from control to guidance. Agree on rules together, such as no phones at the dinner table or after a certain time.

Free tools help: Google Family Link for Android and Screen Time on Apple devices let you approve apps, set time limits and see usage. Most social media platforms set minimum ages in their terms, often 13, so check before your child signs up.

Online gaming risks

  • Chat with strangers: many games have voice and text chat. Restrict chat to friends for younger children.
  • In game purchases: set purchase approvals and do not save cards on kids' accounts. Scams promise free coins or skins in exchange for login details.
  • Fake mods and cheats: downloading cheat tools from random links often installs malware.
  • Account theft: kids share passwords with friends. Teach them, like OTPs, passwords stay private.

Social media and oversharing

Teach children to avoid posting:

  • School name with uniform photos, home address, location tags in real time.
  • Phone numbers and family details.
  • Photos they would not want a stranger or a future employer to see.

Set accounts to private, review follower lists together, and turn off location sharing in apps.

Warning signs of grooming or exploitation

Online predators often pretend to be someone of the same age, give compliments and gifts like game credits, and slowly ask for secrecy and photos. Warning signs in a child:

  • Secretive about a new online friend.
  • Gifts or game currency from unknown people.
  • Sudden anxiety around their phone, or switching screens quickly when you come near.
  • Mentions of an online friend asking them to keep secrets.

Teach a simple rule: anyone online who asks you to keep a secret from your parents, or asks for photos, is not a friend. They should tell you immediately, and it is never the child's fault.

If something bad has happened

If a child is being threatened with photos or videos (sextortion), or has been shown or sent abusive material:

  1. Stay calm and reassure the child that they are not in trouble.
  2. Do not pay or negotiate with the person threatening them.
  3. Save evidence such as usernames and screenshots of messages, but do not save, forward or share any sexual images of a child, because doing that is itself illegal.
  4. Report at cybercrime.gov.in, which has a dedicated section for women and child related crimes with sensitive reporting options, and contact the police.
  5. Report the account to the platform.

Learn together

Make safety a family activity. Play Scam ya Nahi? together, watch an awareness video, or explore the free child friendly material on the government's ISEA awareness portal. Kids often teach parents a thing or two about apps, and parents can teach kids about trust and judgement. Dono milke, internet safe.

Frequently asked questions

What is the right age for a first smartphone? There is no single answer. Many families start with a shared or limited device, then increase independence step by step as the child shows responsibility. Rules matter more than the exact age.

Should I read my teenager's chats? Constant secret monitoring can damage trust. Agree on rules openly, explain the risks, and keep the door open for them to come to you. Step in more closely if you see warning signs.

Are kids' versions of apps fully safe? They help a lot, but no filter is perfect. Supervision and conversation are still needed.

Key takeaways

  • Conversation and trust matter more than any app.
  • Use built in parental controls and age appropriate settings.
  • Teach kids about strangers, oversharing and in game payment scams.
  • Report abuse or exploitation at cybercrime.gov.in, which offers sensitive reporting options.
