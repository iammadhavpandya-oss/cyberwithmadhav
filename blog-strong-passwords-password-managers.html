Strong Passwords and Password Managers: A Simple Guide for Everyone

Most account hacks do not need genius hackers. They use passwords that are short, guessable or reused from another site that was leaked. Here is a simple way to fix that for good.

Quick answer

Use long passphrases (four or more random words), a different password for every important account, and a password manager to remember them. Turn on two factor authentication for email, banking and social media. Change a password immediately if a site you use was breached, and never share passwords over chat or phone.

Why weak and reused passwords get hacked

Attackers rarely sit and guess your password by hand. They use automated tools:

  • Credential stuffing: when one website is breached, attackers try the leaked email and password combinations on other sites. If you reused the password, they get in.
  • Guessing common passwords: passwords like "123456", "password", names plus birth years, or cricket team names are tried first.
  • Phishing: fake login pages trick you into typing your password.

Reuse is the biggest problem. One leak from a small shopping site can open your email, and your email can reset every other account.

The passphrase method

Modern guidance, including NIST's digital identity guidelines, focuses on password length and on blocking known compromised passwords, rather than forcing odd symbols and frequent changes.

A practical way to make a strong, memorable password is a passphrase: four or more random words, for example Tiffin-Monsoon-Guitar-Planet (do not use this exact one). It is long, hard to guess and easy to remember. Avoid famous quotes, song lyrics or personal details like names and birthdays.

Test the idea with the password strength checker on the home page. It runs in your browser, but as a habit, do not type your real password into any website's checker, just try a similar pattern.

What is a password manager?

A password manager is an app that stores all your passwords in an encrypted vault. You remember one strong master password, and the manager remembers the rest. It can also generate random passwords and fill them in automatically on the right website, which helps protect against fake lookalike sites.

Options include password managers built into Google, Apple and Microsoft accounts, browser managers, and dedicated apps. Many have free tiers. Choose a well known one, protect it with a strong master password and two factor authentication, and you are already ahead of most people.

How to switch without stress

Do not try to change 100 passwords in one day. Go in order of importance:

  1. Email (the master key to everything)
  2. Banking and UPI related accounts
  3. Phone account (Google or Apple ID)
  4. Social media and WhatsApp
  5. Shopping and other sites, slowly, as you log in to each

For each one, generate a new unique password in your manager and turn on two factor authentication. Our 2FA guide explains how.

Check if you were in a breach

Websites like Have I Been Pwned let you check whether your email address appeared in known data breaches. If it did, change the password for that site and anywhere you reused it. Many password managers also warn you about reused or breached passwords automatically.

Things to stop doing today

  • Writing passwords on paper stuck to your laptop.
  • Sharing passwords on WhatsApp, even with family. Use the manager's secure sharing feature if needed.
  • Saving passwords in a notes app or an unprotected spreadsheet.
  • Using the same password for office and personal accounts.
  • Telling anyone your password over the phone, whoever they claim to be.

Beyond passwords: passkeys

Many big services now support passkeys, which let you sign in with your phone's fingerprint, face or screen lock instead of a password, and they resist phishing by design. Where available, they are worth turning on. Read our passkeys 2026 explainer. Until every site supports them, a password manager plus unique passwords is your best defence.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to keep all passwords in one place? A reputable password manager encrypts your vault, and it is far safer than reusing passwords or writing them in a notes app. Protect the manager with a strong master password and two factor authentication.

Should I change my passwords every month? Modern guidance, such as NIST's, discourages forced regular changes without a reason, because people then choose weaker patterns. Change a password when there is a sign of compromise or a breach.

What if I forget my master password? Many managers cannot recover it, by design. Set up the recovery options offered when you create the vault, such as a recovery key, and store it safely offline.

Is the browser's built in password manager good enough? For many people, yes, especially when it is tied to an account protected with two factor authentication. The most important thing is that every password is unique.

Key takeaways

  • Length beats complexity: a long passphrase is stronger and easier to remember.
  • Never reuse passwords, especially for email and banking.
  • A password manager stores unique passwords safely for you.
  • Add two factor authentication or passkeys wherever you can.
