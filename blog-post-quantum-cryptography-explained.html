Post-Quantum Cryptography Explained: Why Encryption Is Changing Before Quantum Computers Arrive

Quantum computers that can break today's encryption do not exist yet. So why are governments and big tech changing encryption now? Because data stolen today could be decrypted tomorrow. Here is the simple version.

Quick answer

A large enough quantum computer could break widely used public key algorithms like RSA and elliptic curve cryptography. Attackers can harvest encrypted data now and decrypt it later. NIST published three post-quantum standards in August 2024 (FIPS 203, 204 and 205), and on 27 October 2026 released NISTIR 8547, its transition road map. Organisations should inventory their cryptography and plan migration now.

Why worry about something that does not exist yet?

Most secure communication today, such as HTTPS, VPNs and digital signatures, depends on public key algorithms like RSA and elliptic curve cryptography. These are safe against normal computers. But researchers have known for decades that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could solve the maths behind them much faster.

NIST calls such a machine a cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer. Nobody has one today. The concern is timing: changing cryptography across the world's systems takes many years, so the work must start long before the threat arrives.

Harvest now, decrypt later

Imagine an attacker records encrypted traffic from a bank, a hospital or a government network today. They cannot read it now. But if they store it for ten or fifteen years and a capable quantum computer appears, they might decrypt it then. NIST's October 2026 announcement describes this "harvest now, decrypt later" threat as one of the main reasons the transition is urgent.

Not all data matters in ten years. Your food delivery order does not. But medical records, government secrets, long term business plans and identity data might. That is why sensitive, long lived data gets priority.

The new standards

In August 2024, NIST approved three post-quantum Federal Information Processing Standards:

  • FIPS 203, ML-KEM: for key establishment, the part of encryption where two sides agree on a secret key.
  • FIPS 204, ML-DSA: for digital signatures.
  • FIPS 205, SLH-DSA: a different, hash based approach to digital signatures.

These are designed to resist attacks from both normal and quantum computers.

The transition timeline

On 27 October 2026, NIST released NISTIR 8547, Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards, its high level road map. According to NIST's announcement:

  • US National Security Memorandum 10 set 2035 as the primary target for completing migration across federal systems.
  • An executive order accelerated this for US federal high value assets and high impact systems, to 2030 for key establishment and 2031 for digital signatures.
  • NIST suggests prioritising migration to FIPS 203 for particularly sensitive or long lived data because of harvest now, decrypt later.
  • It also discusses a hybrid approach, combining conventional and post-quantum algorithms, like using two padlocks instead of one.

These timelines are for US federal systems, but they shape what vendors, cloud providers and banks worldwide do, including in India.

What about India?

CERT-In has published technical guidelines on bills of materials that include a QBOM and CBOM (often understood as quantum and cryptographic bills of materials) alongside SBOM, listed on its website as "Technical Guidelines on SBOM, QBOM & CBOM, AIBOM and HBOM Version 2.0". The basic idea of a cryptography inventory, knowing which algorithms are used where, is exactly the first step NIST also emphasises.

What organisations should do now

  1. Inventory cryptography: which systems use RSA, elliptic curves and other public key algorithms, including in vendor products.
  2. Prioritise: sensitive, long lived data and systems that are hard to update.
  3. Build crypto agility: design systems so algorithms can be swapped without rebuilding everything.
  4. Talk to vendors about their PQC road maps.
  5. Test hybrid modes where supported, such as in modern TLS libraries and browsers.

Do individuals need to do anything?

Mostly no. The changes will arrive through software updates in browsers, operating systems, messaging apps and banking apps. Your job is the usual one: keep devices and apps updated. For learners, PQC is a great topic to understand at a conceptual level, and our password hashing post is a good warm up on how cryptographic building blocks differ.

Frequently asked questions

Will quantum computers break all encryption? Not equally. The biggest risk is to public key algorithms like RSA and elliptic curve cryptography. Symmetric encryption like AES and hash functions are considered much less affected, generally needing larger key or output sizes rather than replacement.

When will a cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer exist? Nobody can say with certainty. That uncertainty, plus the long time migrations take, is why NIST and governments are planning now.

Key takeaways

  • Future quantum computers threaten RSA and elliptic curve public key cryptography.
  • Harvest now, decrypt later makes the risk real today for long lived secrets.
  • NIST published PQC standards in 2024 and a transition report in October 2026.
  • The first step for organisations is a cryptography inventory and crypto agility.
