## why-sms | Why SMS scams work so well
SMS feels official. Bank alerts, OTPs and delivery updates all come by SMS, so a fake one blends in. Scammers also know that many people read messages quickly on a small screen, while busy, and act before thinking.

The goal of almost every phishing SMS is one of three things: get you to **open a fake website** that steals your login or card, get you to **call a fake helpline** where someone talks you into paying, or get you to **install an app** that gives them control of your phone.

## common-types | Common fake SMS you will see
- **KYC update:** "Dear customer, your bank account will be blocked today. Update KYC at this link." Banks do not block accounts through an SMS link.
- **Electricity bill:** "Your power connection will be disconnected tonight at 9:30 pm as previous bill was not updated. Call this officer." The number goes to a scammer, who may ask you to install a screen sharing app.
- **Parcel held:** "Your parcel is on hold due to incomplete address. Pay a small fee." The small fee page steals card details.
- **Reward points expiring:** "Your credit card reward points worth a large amount expire today. Redeem now."
- **Traffic challan:** fake e-challan links that look like government sites.
- **Job or part time income:** "Earn from home by liking videos." This usually ends in a task scam.

## red-flags | Five second red flag check
Teach your family these five questions:

1. **Is it scaring me or tempting me?** Fear (block, cut, penalty, arrest) or greed (prize, refund, cashback) is a warning sign.
2. **Is there a link or a phone number to act on?** Genuine organisations want you to use their official app or website.
3. **Is it asking for OTP, PIN, CVV or a password?** That is always a scam.
4. **Does the link look odd?** Spelling mistakes, extra words like "-kyc-update", or short links.
5. **Was I expecting this?** If you did not order a parcel or apply for anything, be extra careful.

If even one answer worries you, do nothing from the message itself.

## sender | Can I trust the sender name?
Legitimate business SMS in India come from registered sender headers, often shown as letters with a short suffix. This helps, but do not treat the header alone as proof. Scammers also send messages from ordinary 10 digit mobile numbers, and they keep inventing new tricks. The safest habit is the same every time: **verify independently.** Open your bank app, the official electricity board website, or the courier's official app, which you find yourself, not from the SMS.

## what-to-do | What to do with a suspicious SMS
- Do not click the link, do not call the number, do not reply.
- Report it on **Chakshu** in the [Sanchar Saathi](https://sancharsaathi.gov.in/) portal or app run by the Department of Telecommunications. Your report helps block numbers that target others too.
- Delete the message after reporting.
- If you want to check a link without opening it, use a link checker such as the [free one on this site](index.html#link-tool), and still do not enter any details.
- Tell family members about the pattern. A two line WhatsApp to your family group can save someone.

## if-clicked | If you already clicked or shared something
Stay calm and act quickly:

1. If you entered card, UPI or bank details, call your bank on the number printed on your card or in the official app and block the card or UPI.
2. If you installed an app on a caller's instruction, turn on airplane mode, uninstall the app, and change your banking and email passwords from another device.
3. If money left your account, call **[1930](tel:1930)** immediately and then file a complaint at [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in/). Our [first hour guide](blog-first-hour-after-cyber-fraud.html) has the full checklist.

## practise | Practise with your family
Spotting scams is a skill, and skills need practice. Sit with your parents for 10 minutes and play [Scam ya Nahi?](game.html), our free spot the scam game in Hinglish. Show them two or three real scam messages from your own phone. Make a family rule: **"Koi bhi link ho, pehle mujhe forward karo."** It sounds simple, but it works.

## rule-1-3 | Rules 1 to 3: the PIN rules
**1. PIN means money is going out.** Your UPI PIN is like your signature on a cheque. You type it only when you are paying. Anyone who says "enter your PIN to receive the refund" is lying.

**2. Never share your PIN, OTP or card details.** Not with family friends on the phone, not with "bank officers", not with "customer care". Real bank staff will never ask for them.

**3. Do not scan QR codes to receive money.** A QR code is for paying. If a buyer on an online marketplace sends you a QR code "to receive the advance", scanning it will send money from your account.

## rule-4-6 | Rules 4 to 6: check before you pay
**4. Read the payee name.** On the final confirmation screen, check that the name matches the shop or person. Fake stickers pasted over a shop's QR code are a known trick.

**5. Decline payment requests you did not start.** NPCI asked banks and UPI apps to stop person to person collect requests from 1 October 2025, but merchant requests still exist. If a payment request pops up and you did not start it, decline it.

**6. Be suspicious of "sent by mistake".** If someone calls saying they sent money to you by mistake, first check your bank app, not the SMS, because fake credit SMS are common. If money really came, ask them to contact their bank, which handles wrong transfers properly.

## rule-7-8 | Rules 7 and 8: protect the phone
**7. No screen sharing or remote access apps on request.** Apps that let someone see or control your screen are useful for genuine IT support, but scammers use them to watch you type your PIN. If a caller asks you to install one, hang up.

**8. Lock and update.** Use a screen lock, set an app lock on UPI apps if available, and keep your phone's operating system and apps updated. Install apps only from the official Play Store or App Store.

## rule-9-10 | Rules 9 and 10: limits and alerts
**9. Turn on alerts and check them.** Keep SMS and app notifications for every debit. If you see a payment you did not make, act immediately.

**10. Set sensible limits.** Many banking and UPI apps let you set a daily transaction limit. For elderly parents who make small payments, a lower limit reduces the damage if something goes wrong. Use a separate account with a small balance for daily UPI if that suits you.

## support | Finding real customer support
A very common trap is searching online for "XYZ bank customer care number" and calling the first result. Scammers post fake numbers in search results, social media comments and fake websites. Always take support contacts from:

- the official app (help or support section)
- the back of your debit or credit card
- the official website, typed by you

The Sanchar Saathi portal also offers a trusted contact details service for verifying bank customer care numbers.

## liability | If money is lost: speed and your rights
Act in this order: call **[1930](tel:1930)**, inform your bank and block the affected card or UPI, then complete the complaint on [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in/) with every transaction ID.

The RBI circular of 6 July 2017 on customer liability says that when an unauthorised electronic transaction happens because of a third party breach (not your negligence), and you report it to your bank within three working days, your liability is zero. Reporting later can increase your liability, so speed matters. If the loss happened because you shared your PIN or OTP, the rules are different, which is one more reason never to share them.

## family | Make it a family habit
Print or write these rules on one page and stick it near where your parents keep their phone. Practise with them: "If someone calls and says enter PIN to get refund, what will you do?" The right answer is "Call cut karke beta ko phone karungi." For more examples, read our detailed guide to [UPI scams](blog-upi-scams-india.html).

quick: Most WhatsApp takeovers happen when someone tricks you into sharing the **six digit registration code** sent by SMS, or gets your account opened on a **linked device**. Turn on **two step verification** (a PIN that WhatsApp asks for when your number is registered on a new phone), add an email for recovery, and regularly check **Linked devices**. takeaways:
- The WhatsApp registration code is like a house key. Never share it.
- Two step verification adds a PIN that stops most takeovers.
- Check linked devices regularly and log out of any you do not recognise.

## how | How takeovers usually happen
**The code trick.** Someone tries to register your number on their phone. WhatsApp sends a six digit code to your SMS. Then a message arrives, often from a friend's already hacked account: "Sorry, I sent my code to your number by mistake, please forward it." The moment you share it, your account moves to their phone.

**Fake support or prize messages.** Messages pretending to be WhatsApp support or a contest ask for the code "to verify" you.

**Linked devices.** WhatsApp Web and desktop work by scanning a QR code with your phone. If someone gets your unlocked phone for a minute, or tricks you into scanning their QR code, they can read your chats from their computer.

**Voicemail tricks.** In some cases attackers request the code by voice call and try to access voicemail. Setting a voicemail PIN with your operator helps.

## protect | Protect your account in two minutes
1. Open WhatsApp **Settings**, go to **Account**, then **Two step verification**, and turn it on. Choose a six digit PIN that is not your birthday.
2. Add an **email address** when asked, so you can reset the PIN if you forget it.
3. Go to **Linked devices** and log out of anything you do not recognise.
4. Keep your phone locked with a strong screen lock.
5. Turn on **Security notifications** so you know when something changes.

Menu names can change slightly with app updates, so if you cannot find an option, check the official WhatsApp Help Center.

## signs | Signs your account may be compromised
- You are suddenly logged out with a message that your number is registered on another phone.
- Friends ask why you sent strange messages or money requests.
- You see unknown sessions in Linked devices.
- Chats are marked as read before you open them.

## recover | If your WhatsApp is taken over
1. **Re register immediately.** Open WhatsApp on your phone and verify your number with the SMS code. This logs the other person out. If they set a two step PIN, you may have to wait before you can register again, but they also lose access.
2. **Warn your contacts** through other channels: calls, SMS or another app. Tell them not to send money to anyone using your name.
3. **Email WhatsApp support** from the app or Help Center, explaining your account was stolen, with your number in international format.
4. **Report** the incident on [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in/), especially if money was requested or lost. If anyone lost money, they should call [1930](tel:1930).
5. **Check your other accounts.** If the attacker had your phone or email, change those passwords too.

## money-requests | When a friend asks for urgent money on WhatsApp
Hacked accounts are used to send messages like "Urgent, in hospital, please send 20,000 on this UPI, will return tomorrow." The message looks real because it comes from your friend's real account. Always call the person on their normal number, ideally a voice call where you can hear them speak naturally. With AI voice cloning becoming common, ask a question only the real person would know. Our post on [AI voice cloning scams](blog-ai-voice-cloning-deepfake-scams-2026.html) explains why.

## india-rules | What is changing in India
The Department of Telecommunications issued directions on 28 November 2025 requiring messaging apps that use Indian mobile numbers to keep accounts continuously linked to the active SIM in the device and to log out web sessions periodically. Reports in October 2026 said enforcement is planned from 1 January 2027. We explain what this means for you in our [SIM binding explainer](blog-sim-binding-whatsapp-india-2027.html). Rules help, but your two step PIN is still your strongest personal defence.

## habit | One habit to teach everyone
**"Code kisi ko nahi dena. Kabhi nahi."** Not to a friend, not to support, not to a cousin. WhatsApp itself will never ask you for this code in a chat. Teach it to parents and kids in the same simple words.

## what-otp | What an OTP really does
OTP means one time password. It is sent to your registered phone or email to confirm that **you** are approving something right now. Banks use it for payments and new beneficiaries, apps use it for logins and new devices, and telecom operators use it for SIM related requests.

If someone else has your card number or password, the OTP is often the last lock. That is why scammers want it so badly. Giving it away is like handing over the last key to your locker.

## stories | The stories scammers tell
- **"I am calling from your bank."** "Your card will be blocked, we are sending an OTP to verify." The OTP actually approves a payment or a new device.
- **"Delivery agent here."** "Tell me the OTP to confirm your parcel." Sometimes it is real for some delivery services, so check: did you order something, and does the SMS clearly say it is a delivery code from that company?
- **"SIM upgrade or KYC from your telecom operator."** The OTP may approve a SIM swap, after which your calls and OTPs go to the scammer.
- **"Refund or cashback."** "To process your refund, tell me the OTP." Refunds never need your OTP.
- **"Wrong OTP sent to you."** A stranger or a friend's hacked account asks you to forward a code. It may be your WhatsApp or another account's registration code.

## read-sms | Read the OTP message before anything
Most genuine OTP messages say what they are for, for example "OTP for transaction of a certain amount at a certain merchant" or "OTP to log in". They also often say "Do not share this OTP with anyone". Take five seconds to read the whole SMS. If it says payment and you are not paying, stop. If it says login and you are not logging in, someone has your password.

## delivery | The delivery OTP exception
Some delivery services do use OTPs to confirm handover of a package. The rule here: share it only **at your door, with the agent physically there, for a parcel you actually ordered**, and only if the SMS clearly comes from that delivery service. Never share it over the phone with someone who calls in advance. If in doubt, check the order in the official app.

## shared | If you shared an OTP by mistake
1. **Call your bank immediately** using the number on your card or official app. Ask them to block cards, UPI or net banking as needed.
2. **Change passwords** of the related account, starting with email and banking.
3. **Check for a SIM swap.** If your phone suddenly loses network, contact your operator at once from another phone.
4. **Call [1930](tel:1930)** if money moved, and file at [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in/).
5. **Report the number** on Sanchar Saathi Chakshu.

Remember that under the RBI's 2017 customer liability rules, losses caused by sharing your own payment credentials are treated differently from third party breaches, so prevention is far better than cure.

## sim-swap | SIM swap: when OTPs go to the scammer
In a SIM swap, a criminal convinces or tricks a telecom outlet into issuing a new SIM for your number. Your phone loses signal, and all OTPs start going to them. Warning signs: sudden "no service" for a long time while others nearby have network, or messages about a SIM change you did not request. Act fast: call your operator from another phone and your bank. Use the Sanchar Saathi portal to check which mobile connections are registered in your name and report ones that are not yours.

## family-rule | The family rule
Write it big: **"OTP sirf apne liye. Kisi ko bolna nahi, forward karna nahi."** Practise with parents using simple role play. Most OTP frauds work on people who were never told this clearly even once.

## types | The common types of job scams
- **Task based part time jobs.** "Earn 3,000 to 5,000 daily by liking YouTube videos or reviewing hotels." You get paid small amounts first, then you are added to a Telegram group and asked to "invest" for bigger tasks. The money you put in never comes out.
- **Fake offer letters.** A well designed offer letter with a famous company's logo, followed by a request for a fee for "background verification", "laptop" or "training".
- **Fake recruitment agencies.** Promises of jobs abroad or in big companies for a large consultancy fee.
- **Data entry and form filling.** You sign a "contract", fail to meet impossible accuracy targets, and get threatened with a "legal penalty" unless you pay.

## task-scam | How the task scam hooks people
The task scam is clever because it pays you at first. Day one, you like three videos and receive a small amount in your bank account. Now you trust it. Then comes a "prepaid task" or "merchant task" where you must deposit money to earn a higher commission. The dashboard shows big profits, but to withdraw, you are told to pay a "tax" or "unlock fee". Each payment leads to another demand.

If you are in this loop now, the most important thing is: **stop paying.** No additional payment will release your money.

## red-flags | Warning signs of a fake job
- No interview, or only a chat on WhatsApp or Telegram.
- Recruiter uses a personal email ID or a free email for a big company name.
- Salary far higher than normal for the work.
- Pressure to decide and pay quickly.
- Any fee: registration, training, uniform, laptop, verification or "refundable deposit".
- Requests for your bank login, UPI PIN or OTP "to deposit salary".

## verify | How to verify a job offer
1. Find the company's official website yourself and check its careers page for the role.
2. Look up the recruiter on LinkedIn: real profile, real connections, real history.
3. Check the email domain. A real company usually uses its own domain.
4. Call the company's official number from its website and ask HR to confirm.
5. Ask a senior, teacher or friend in the industry. A second opinion breaks the pressure.

## mule | Do not become a money mule
Some fake jobs ask you to "receive payments" into your account and forward them, or to open bank accounts and share the kit. This makes you a **money mule**, helping criminals move stolen money. Your account can be frozen and you can face legal trouble. Never lend your bank account, UPI or SIM to anyone for a "job".

## paid | If you already paid
1. Stop all further payments and stop replying.
2. Take screenshots of chats, the group, the website, payment receipts and UPI IDs.
3. Call **[1930](tel:1930)** quickly, then file at [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in/) with every transaction ID.
4. Inform your bank.
5. Report the numbers on Sanchar Saathi Chakshu and report the group or channel inside the app.

Do not trust anyone who later contacts you offering to "recover" your money for a fee. That is usually a second scam.

## mindset | The right mindset
There is no shame in being targeted. These groups run like businesses and are very convincing. Talk about it openly with friends and family, especially younger cousins looking for their first job. **"Naukri ke liye paise maange, matlab fraud."** Simple rule, saves a lot of pain.
