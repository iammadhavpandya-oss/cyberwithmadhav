## simple-meaning | Cybersecurity in one simple picture
Think of your phone. It holds your photos, your chats, your UPI app and your email. Now imagine three things a criminal might want: to **read** your private stuff, to **change** something (like the account number on a payment), or to **lock you out** so you cannot use it at all. Cybersecurity is everything we do to stop those three things.

At a company, the same idea is just bigger. Instead of one phone, there are thousands of laptops, servers, cloud accounts and customer records. Instead of one person, there are teams whose full time job is protecting them.

So when someone says "I work in cyber", they could be configuring firewalls, watching alerts in a security operations centre, testing apps for bugs, writing policies, investigating a hacked laptop or teaching staff how to spot phishing. It is a very wide field, and that is good news for beginners, because there is a place for many kinds of people.

## cia-triad | The CIA triad: the three things we protect
Security people use a short model called the **CIA triad**. It has nothing to do with the American agency.

- **Confidentiality** means only the right people can see the data. Example: your bank statement should be visible to you and the bank, not to a stranger on the same WiFi.
- **Integrity** means data is not changed without permission. Example: an invoice should not have its bank details quietly swapped by an attacker.
- **Availability** means the system works when you need it. Example: a hospital system should not go down because of ransomware during an emergency.

Whenever you learn a new attack or defence, ask yourself: which of these three does it hit or protect? This one habit will make concepts much easier to remember.

## common-attacks | Common attacks you will hear about
You will meet these names again and again in this series:

- **Phishing:** fake emails, SMS or WhatsApp messages that trick you into clicking a link or sharing an OTP.
- **Malware:** harmful software, such as spyware that reads your messages or ransomware that encrypts files and demands money.
- **Password attacks:** guessing or reusing leaked passwords to break into accounts.
- **Exploiting vulnerabilities:** using a bug in software that has not been updated.
- **Social engineering:** manipulating people, for example by pretending to be police, bank staff or a boss.

Notice that many of these target humans, not machines. That is why awareness matters as much as technology. Our [Scam Encyclopedia](scams.html) shows how these ideas look in real Indian scams.

## defenders | What defenders actually do
Defence is usually described in layers, because no single control is perfect:

1. **Prevent:** strong passwords, two factor authentication, updates, firewalls and least privilege (give each person only the access they need).
2. **Detect:** logs, alerts and monitoring tools that notice unusual activity, like a login from a new country at 3 am.
3. **Respond:** contain the problem, remove the attacker, fix the root cause.
4. **Recover:** restore from backups and get back to normal.

Most beginners imagine cybersecurity as only "hacking". In reality, a large share of the jobs are on the defensive side, often called the **blue team**. Attack simulation roles, the **red team** and penetration testers, are exciting but they also need strong defensive knowledge.

## mindset | The beginner mindset that works
Here is the honest truth: there is no shortcut and no magic tool. People who do well in this field usually share a few habits.

- They are curious and ask "how does this actually work?"
- They practise a little every day instead of binge watching tutorials once a month.
- They write notes in their own words.
- They stay strictly legal. Testing a website, WiFi network or app that you do not own, without written permission, can be a crime under the IT Act in India.

If you are coming from a non technical background, such as commerce, arts or law, you are not behind. Communication, writing and understanding rules are real strengths in security work.

## why-map | Why you need a map first
Many freshers watch one video on ethical hacking and decide they want to be a hacker. Then they jump between Kali tools, bug bounty, cloud and malware analysis, and after six months they feel lost. The problem is not effort. The problem is direction.

The US based NICE Framework, published by NIST, lists dozens of distinct cybersecurity work roles. You do not need to memorise it, but it shows one important thing: security is a whole industry with many specialisations. Let us group them into a few families you can actually understand.

## defensive | Defensive roles (blue team)
These people protect and monitor systems.

- **SOC analyst:** watches alerts from tools like SIEM and EDR, checks if they are real attacks and escalates. This is one of the most common first jobs. Part 12 of this series covers a typical day.
- **Incident responder:** steps in when an attack is confirmed, contains it and figures out what happened.
- **Threat hunter:** proactively searches logs for signs of attackers that tools missed.
- **Security engineer:** builds and tunes defences such as firewalls, email filters and endpoint tools.

Good fit if you like patterns, logs, puzzles and calm problem solving.

## offensive | Offensive roles (red team)
These people think like attackers, with permission.

- **Penetration tester:** tests apps, networks or cloud setups under a signed scope and writes a report.
- **Red teamer:** simulates a real attacker over weeks to test people, process and technology together.
- **Bug bounty hunter:** finds bugs in programs that invite researchers, and gets rewarded under the program rules.

Good fit if you enjoy breaking things, reading code and writing clear reports. Honest note: offensive roles usually need more experience and a strong portfolio. The report writing part is as important as the hacking part.

## cloud-app | Cloud and application security
Most companies now run on cloud platforms like AWS, Azure and Google Cloud, and build their own web and mobile apps.

- **Cloud security engineer:** secures cloud accounts, identities, storage and networks. Part 13 introduces the basics.
- **Application security (AppSec):** reviews code, runs scanners and helps developers fix bugs early.
- **DevSecOps:** adds security checks into the build and deployment pipeline.

Good fit if you like building things and have some coding or cloud interest.

## grc-forensics | GRC, privacy and forensics
Not every security job is deeply technical, and these roles are growing in India.

- **GRC analyst:** works on policies, risk assessments, audits and standards like ISO 27001.
- **Privacy and data protection:** helps companies follow laws like India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. People with a law or commerce background can do very well here.
- **Digital forensics:** collects and analyses evidence from devices, often supporting investigations.

Good fit if you are organised, enjoy writing, and like understanding rules and how businesses work.

## choose | How to choose your first direction
Ask yourself three simple questions:

1. Do I enjoy logs and puzzles (SOC), building and configuring (engineering or cloud), or reading and writing rules (GRC)?
2. What do I already have? A networking background points to SOC or network security. A coding background points to AppSec. A law degree points to GRC and privacy.
3. What can I prove in 90 days? A small home lab, a few CTF writeups, or a sample risk assessment.

Pick one path for the next six months. You can always switch later, and the basics carry over. Mera suggestion: if you are fully confused, start with the SOC track, because it teaches networking, Linux, Windows and attacks together. The [90 day study plan](blog-90-day-cybersecurity-study-plan.html) gives you a ready schedule.

## reality-check | A quick reality check
Job titles vary a lot between companies. One company's "security analyst" is another company's "SOC L1". Read the actual responsibilities in job posts, not just the title. Also be careful with anyone who promises a guaranteed cybersecurity job after a short paid course. Real hiring depends on skills you can show, not on a certificate alone.

## ip | IP addresses: the address of a device
Every device on a network gets an **IP address**. The common version, IPv4, looks like `192.168.1.10`: four numbers from 0 to 255 separated by dots.

There are two kinds you must know:

- **Private IPs** are used inside homes and offices. Ranges like `10.x.x.x`, `172.16.x.x to 172.31.x.x` and `192.168.x.x` are reserved for private use.
- **Public IPs** are what the internet sees. Your home router usually has one public IP, and all your devices share it.

There is also **IPv6**, which looks like `2001:db8::1`. It exists because the world ran out of IPv4 addresses. You will see both in logs.

## subnet | Subnets in simple words
A **subnet** is a group of addresses that belong together, like all flats on one floor. You will see notation like `192.168.1.0/24`. The `/24` means the first 24 bits are the network part, which gives 256 addresses, of which 254 are usable for devices.

Why does this matter for security? Because firewalls rules, scanning scope and incident reports are all written in subnets. If an alert says "traffic from 10.0.5.0/24", you should know that means a whole range, not one machine. Practise with the free [subnet calculator on the home page](index.html#subnet) until it feels natural.

## ports | Ports and protocols
One device can run many services. **Ports** tell traffic which service it is meant for. Think of the IP as a building and the port as a flat number.

Common ports to remember:

- **22** SSH, remote login to Linux servers
- **53** DNS, name lookups
- **80** HTTP, unencrypted web
- **443** HTTPS, encrypted web
- **3389** RDP, Windows remote desktop
- **25 and 587** email sending (SMTP)

The two main transport protocols are **TCP**, which is reliable and connection based (web, email, SSH), and **UDP**, which is faster and connectionless (DNS lookups, video calls, games). An open port means a service is listening. An open port that should not be open, like RDP exposed to the whole internet, is a classic way attackers get in.

## dns | DNS: the internet's phonebook
Humans remember names, computers use numbers. **DNS** (Domain Name System) translates `www.example.com` into an IP address.

Attackers love DNS. Phishing sites use lookalike domains such as an extra letter or a different ending. Malware sometimes talks to its controller using DNS. That is why SOC analysts often check DNS logs first when investigating a suspicious laptop. In India, banks moved their websites to the special `.bank.in` domain, which is a DNS level trust signal. We explain that in [our .bank.in guide](blog-bank-in-domain-india.html).

## devices | Routers, switches and firewalls
- **Switch:** connects devices inside the same local network.
- **Router:** connects different networks, for example your home network to your internet provider.
- **Firewall:** a gatekeeper that allows or blocks traffic based on rules, like "allow web traffic out, block remote desktop in".
- **NAT** (network address translation): lets many private devices share one public IP. It is why your laptop's `192.168.x.x` address is not directly reachable from the internet.

Your home WiFi router is usually all of these in one small box. That is a nice place to start learning. Log in to its admin page, look at connected devices, and change the default admin password if nobody did.

## practise | How to practise networking safely
- Run `ipconfig` (Windows) or `ip a` (Linux) and find your own IP, subnet and gateway.
- Run `nslookup example.com` and see the IP that DNS returns.
- Install Wireshark and watch your own traffic while you open a website. Our [Wireshark basics guide](blog-wireshark-basics.html) walks you through it.
- Try the free Cisco Networking Academy courses for structured learning.

Only look at traffic on your own network and devices. Capturing other people's traffic without permission is not okay, legally or ethically. Next, in Part 4, we move to Linux, the operating system you will use most in security work.

## why-linux | Why Linux matters so much
When you work in security, you will meet Linux everywhere. Web servers, cloud instances, firewalls, routers and many security tools run on it. Attackers also love Linux servers because they often hold valuable data.

Many beginners install Kali Linux on day one and feel like hackers. Kali is useful, but it is just a Linux distribution with security tools pre installed. If you do not understand basic Linux, Kali will only confuse you. Start with a normal distribution like Ubuntu, learn the basics, and then explore Kali in your lab.

## navigation | Moving around the file system
Linux organises everything in a tree starting at `/` (root). Key commands:

```
pwd            # where am I?
ls -la         # list files, including hidden ones
cd /var/log    # go to a folder
cd ~           # go to your home folder
mkdir notes    # make a folder
cp a.txt b.txt # copy
mv a.txt docs/ # move or rename
```

Important folders: `/etc` holds configuration, `/home` holds user folders, `/var/log` holds logs and `/tmp` holds temporary files. Attackers often drop files in `/tmp` because anyone can write there, so defenders check it.

## reading | Reading and searching files
Security work is mostly reading. These commands help:

```
cat file.txt               # print a file
less /var/log/syslog       # scroll through a big file
head -n 20 file.txt        # first 20 lines
tail -f /var/log/auth.log  # follow new lines live
grep "Failed password" /var/log/auth.log
```

`grep` is your best friend. Combined with `|` (pipe), you can filter huge logs in seconds, for example `grep "Failed password" auth.log | wc -l` counts failed SSH logins.

## permissions | Users and permissions
Every file has an owner, a group and permissions. Run `ls -l` and you will see something like `-rwxr-x---`. That means the owner can read, write and execute, the group can read and execute, and others can do nothing.

```
chmod 640 secret.txt   # owner rw, group r, others none
chown madhav file.txt  # change owner
whoami                 # which user am I?
sudo command           # run as administrator
```

Wrong permissions are a common real world weakness, for example a configuration file with a password that every user can read. Be careful with `sudo`. Never paste a command from the internet with sudo unless you understand every part of it.

## processes-network | Processes and networking
```
ps aux        # list running processes
top           # live view of CPU and memory
ip a          # show IP addresses
ss -tulpn     # which ports are listening, and by which program
ping 8.8.8.8  # basic connectivity test
```

`ss -tulpn` is very useful for defenders. If you see a strange program listening on an unusual port, that deserves a closer look. The networking ideas from Part 3 now become real.

## logs | Logs: where the evidence lives
On many Linux systems, logs live in `/var/log`. Files like `auth.log` (or `secure` on some distributions) record logins and sudo use. Newer systems also use `journalctl`. A SOC analyst who can quickly read these logs is valuable. We go deeper in [Linux logs for beginners](blog-linux-logs-for-beginners.html).

- HTTPS encrypts traffic, but a padlock does not prove a site is honest.

## step-dns | Step 1: Finding the address with DNS
You type `www.example.com`. Your computer does not know where that is, so it asks a **DNS resolver**, usually run by your internet provider or a public service. The resolver finds the IP address and replies. Your device caches the answer for a while so the next visit is faster.

**Security angle:** attackers register lookalike domains, for example swapping one letter, to fool people. Some malware also tampers with DNS settings to send you to fake sites. This is why you should always read the domain name carefully, especially for banking. Indian banks now use the `.bank.in` ending, explained in [this guide](blog-bank-in-domain-india.html).

## step-route | Step 2: Routing packets across networks
With the IP address known, your device sends data in small chunks called **packets**. They go to your home router, then your internet provider, then across many routers until they reach the server's network. Each router just looks at the destination IP and forwards the packet closer.

**Security angle:** anyone who controls a network on the path, such as a fake public WiFi hotspot, could try to see or modify unencrypted traffic. This is the reason encryption matters so much.

## step-tcp | Step 3: Opening a TCP connection
Web traffic uses **TCP**, which makes sure data arrives complete and in order. Before sending real data, client and server do a quick greeting called the **three way handshake**: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. After that, the connection is open on the server's port, usually 443 for HTTPS.

**Security angle:** port scanners like nmap use this handshake behaviour to discover which ports are open. Defenders watch for many half finished handshakes, which can signal scanning or a flood attack.

## step-tls | Step 4: TLS, the padlock
For HTTPS, the browser and server now set up **TLS** encryption. The server shows a **certificate** that proves it controls that domain name, signed by a trusted certificate authority. They agree on keys, and from here everything is encrypted.

**Security angle, very important:** the padlock means the connection is encrypted and the domain matches the certificate. It does **not** mean the website is genuine or safe. Phishing sites also get free certificates. A padlock on `sbi-kyc-update-help.com` is still a scam site. Teach this to your family.

## step-http | Step 5: HTTP request and response
Inside the encrypted tunnel, your browser sends an **HTTP request**, like `GET /index.html`, along with headers such as cookies. The server replies with a status code (200 OK, 404 Not Found, 500 Server Error) and the content. Your browser then fetches more files for images, styles and scripts, and builds the page you see.

**Security angle:** cookies often hold your logged in session. If an attacker steals that cookie, they may not need your password. Web attacks such as cross site scripting and SQL injection happen at this layer. You will learn them on practice sites in the [Burp Suite intro](blog-burp-suite-intro-practice.html).

## see-it | See it yourself in five minutes
1. Open any website in Chrome or Firefox.
2. Press F12 to open developer tools and click the **Network** tab.
3. Reload the page. You will see every request, its status code, size and timing.
4. Click the padlock in the address bar and view the certificate details.
5. In a terminal, run `nslookup example.com` to see the DNS step on its own.

This is all on your own browser, so it is completely safe. Once you can explain these five steps to a friend in simple Hindi or English, you have a stronger foundation than many people who only know tool names. Part 6 maps these steps to the OSI and TCP/IP models.

## why-layers | Why layers at all?
Sending a message across the world is complicated. Layers split the problem into pieces, so each piece can be designed and fixed on its own. Your WiFi card does not need to know anything about WhatsApp, and WhatsApp does not need to know whether you are on WiFi or mobile data.

For security, layers give us a common language. If someone in a SOC says "we are seeing a layer 3 flood", or "this is a layer 7 issue", everyone knows roughly where to look.

A popular memory trick, from layer 1 up, is **"Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away"**: Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application.

## lower | Layers 1 to 3: getting bits from place to place
- **Layer 1, Physical:** cables, radio signals, fibre. Attack example: someone plugs a rogue device into an open office network port. Defence: physical security and disabling unused ports.
- **Layer 2, Data Link:** MAC addresses and switches inside a local network. Attack example: ARP spoofing, where an attacker on the same WiFi pretends to be the router to intercept traffic. Defence: switch security features, network segmentation and encrypted protocols.
- **Layer 3, Network:** IP addresses and routing between networks. Attack example: IP spoofing or a flood of packets to overwhelm a link. Defence: firewalls, filtering and upstream DDoS protection.

## middle | Layer 4: Transport
This is where **TCP** and **UDP** live, along with **ports**. TCP gives reliable connections with the three way handshake you saw in Part 5. UDP is quicker but has no handshake.

Attack examples: port scanning to find open services, and SYN floods that open many half connections to exhaust a server. Defence: closing unused ports, firewalls that track connection state, and rate limiting. When you scan your own lab with nmap later in this series, you are working mostly at this layer.

## upper | Layers 5 to 7: sessions, formats and applications
- **Layer 5, Session:** managing ongoing conversations between systems. In the real world, session handling is often done inside applications, for example login sessions using cookies or tokens. Attack example: session hijacking with a stolen cookie.
- **Layer 6, Presentation:** data formats, character encoding and often encryption, such as TLS. Attack example: weak or outdated encryption settings. Defence: modern TLS configuration.
- **Layer 7, Application:** the protocols apps speak, like HTTP, DNS, SMTP and SSH. Attack examples: SQL injection, phishing pages, credential stuffing and API abuse. Defence: secure coding, web application firewalls, strong authentication.

Most of the attacks you read about in news stories live at layer 7, because that is where the valuable data and the humans are.

## tcp-ip | The TCP/IP model the internet actually uses
OSI is a teaching model. The real internet was built on the **TCP/IP model**, described in standards like RFC 1122. It has four layers:

1. **Link:** roughly OSI layers 1 and 2.
2. **Internet:** OSI layer 3, mainly IP.
3. **Transport:** OSI layer 4, TCP and UDP.
4. **Application:** OSI layers 5, 6 and 7 combined.

Interviews often ask "difference between OSI and TCP/IP". A good simple answer: OSI is a seven layer reference model used for teaching and troubleshooting, TCP/IP is the four layer model that real internet protocols follow.

## learn-it | How to really learn the layers
Do not just memorise. Open Wireshark on your own computer, capture a few seconds of traffic while you load a website, and click one packet. You will see Ethernet or WiFi (layer 2), IP (layer 3), TCP (layer 4) and HTTP or TLS (upper layers) stacked like folders. Our [Wireshark basics guide](blog-wireshark-basics.html) shows exactly how.

Make a one page table in your notes: layer, what it does, one attack, one defence. Revise it once a week. Once you understand it, you will not forget it. Next, Part 7: building a safe home lab where you can practise all of this.

## why-lab | Why every beginner needs a lab
Watching videos teaches you words. A lab teaches you skills. In a lab you can run scans, read logs, break a web app and fix it, all without asking anyone's permission, because every machine belongs to you.

The legal point is serious. Scanning or attacking systems you do not own, without written permission, can be an offence under the Information Technology Act, 2000 in India, even if you "were just learning". A lab removes that risk completely.

## hardware | What hardware do you need?
You do not need a gaming PC. A practical minimum:

- **RAM:** 8 GB works for one or two small VMs. 16 GB is comfortable.
- **Storage:** 50 to 100 GB free space, ideally on an SSD.
- **CPU:** any recent processor with virtualization support enabled in BIOS or UEFI (often called Intel VT-x or AMD-V).

If your laptop is weaker, use lightweight Linux VMs, run one VM at a time, or use browser based practice platforms and CTFs for a while.

## setup | Step by step setup
1. **Install a hypervisor.** VirtualBox is free and runs on Windows, Linux and macOS hosts (check the official site for current Apple silicon support). VMware Workstation and Fusion also offer free personal use editions. Pick one.
2. **Create a workstation VM.** Ubuntu is great for learning basics. Kali Linux provides ready made VM images if you want security tools pre installed.
3. **Add a target.** Good beginner options are OWASP Juice Shop (a deliberately insecure web shop, run with Docker), DVWA (Damn Vulnerable Web Application) or vulnerable VMs from trusted training sources.
4. **Configure an isolated network.** In VirtualBox, use a **Host only** adapter or an **Internal Network** for the target. This keeps vulnerable machines off your home WiFi and away from the internet.
5. **Take a snapshot** of each VM in a clean state.

## isolation | Network isolation, explained simply
Intentionally vulnerable machines are, by design, easy to hack. If you connect one in **bridged** mode, it becomes a real device on your home network, visible to everything on your WiFi. In the worst case, a misconfigured setup could expose it further.

The safe rule: the vulnerable target should only be able to talk to your attacker or workstation VM. Give your workstation a second adapter with NAT if it needs internet for updates. Disconnect the target from the internet entirely. Draw a small diagram of your lab network in your notes. Interviewers love it when freshers can explain this.

## first-exercises | Your first five lab exercises
1. Find the IP addresses of both VMs with `ip a` and ping one from the other.
2. Scan your target with nmap and list its open ports. Our [nmap own lab guide](blog-nmap-own-lab-guide.html) explains the commands.
3. Open the target's web app in the workstation browser and explore every page.
4. Capture the traffic between the two VMs with Wireshark.
5. Read the target's logs after your scan and see what your activity looked like from the defender side.

That last step is gold. It teaches you both red and blue thinking at the same time.

## habits | Good lab habits
- Revert to a clean snapshot before each new exercise.
- Keep a lab notebook: what you tried, what worked, what you learned.
- Update your workstation VM regularly.
- Never store real passwords, personal files or work data inside lab VMs.
- Turn VMs off when you are not using them.

Once your lab is ready, you are set for the rest of this journey. Next, Part 8 covers free certifications that are worth your time.

## truth | The honest truth about certificates
A certificate shows that you studied a syllabus and passed a test. It does not prove you can investigate an alert or secure a server. Recruiters know this. Still, for freshers, a recognised beginner certificate can help your resume get past automated filters and show commitment.

The smart approach: use certificates as a **study plan**, and use projects, labs and CTF writeups as **proof**.

## free-paths | Free learning paths worth doing
These are well known providers with free beginner material. Details change, so check each official page:

- **Cisco Networking Academy:** offers free self paced courses such as Introduction to Cybersecurity and networking basics, with a completion badge.
- **Microsoft Learn:** free learning paths for the SC-900 Security, Compliance and Identity Fundamentals exam. The learning is free, the exam usually has a fee, though Microsoft sometimes runs discount or free voucher events.
- **Fortinet Training Institute:** free security awareness and fundamentals level courses.
- **ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity (CC):** an entry level certification. ISC2 has run programs offering free training and a free exam for this certification. Check the official ISC2 page for whether an offer is active and what the annual maintenance fee is.

## cloud | Cloud fundamentals
Cloud skills are in demand, and the big providers have beginner tracks:

- AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud and Oracle Cloud all publish free training for their foundational certifications.
- Oracle has at times offered free foundations exams for Oracle Cloud Infrastructure and related AI tracks. I have done the Oracle Cloud AI Associate myself, so I can say the free learning material is good, but check the official Oracle University site for the current offer before you plan.

A fundamentals certificate plus a small cloud security project, such as locking down a storage bucket in a free tier account, makes a strong pair.

## paid-later | Paid certificates to consider later
Once you have basics and some savings or employer support, popular next steps include CompTIA Security+ and vendor specific security certifications. Hands on practical exams are highly respected in offensive security, but they are not beginner level. Do not buy an expensive exam in month one.

## red-flags | Red flags when choosing a course
- "100 percent job guarantee" after a short course.
- Very high fees for a certificate nobody in job posts mentions.
- No syllabus, no trainer details, no official accreditation information.
- Pressure to pay today because "seats are filling".

A quick test: search for the certificate name in real job posts on LinkedIn or Naukri. If almost nobody asks for it, think twice.

## plan | A simple certificate plan for your first year
1. Months 1 to 3: one free foundations course (networking or intro to cybersecurity).
2. Months 3 to 6: one recognised entry level certificate, ideally free or discounted.
3. Months 6 to 12: one cloud fundamentals certificate, or save up for a respected paid exam.

Alongside, keep building your home lab, doing CTFs and publishing writeups on GitHub. That combination is far more convincing than a long list of certificates. Next, Part 9: CTFs for beginners.
