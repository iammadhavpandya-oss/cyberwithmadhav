Is Public WiFi Safe? How to Use Free WiFi at Airports, Cafes and Stations

Free WiFi at the airport or a cafe is convenient, but you do not know who runs it or who else is on it. You do not need to fear it. You just need a few smart habits.

Quick answer

Public WiFi is fine for browsing news or maps, especially on HTTPS sites, but avoid banking and payments on it when you can and use mobile data instead. Beware of fake hotspots with official sounding names, never install anything a WiFi login page asks for, turn off auto connect, and keep your phone updated. CERT-In advises avoiding public WiFi for sensitive transactions and using a VPN when necessary.

What can go wrong on public WiFi

  • Fake hotspots ("evil twins"). Anyone can create a WiFi network named "Airport_Free_WiFi" or "Cafe_Guest". If you connect to the attacker's network, they control your connection.
  • Snooping on unencrypted traffic. Websites or apps that do not use encryption can expose what you send to others on the same network.
  • Malicious login pages. Some fake captive portals ask you to "install a certificate" or an app, or to enter your email password or card details.
  • Attacks on your device. On a shared network, other devices can try to reach yours, which is a bigger risk if your system is outdated or file sharing is open.

Why HTTPS helps, and its limits

Today most websites and apps use HTTPS, which encrypts the content between your device and the site. That means a snooper on the cafe WiFi usually cannot read your password or messages on those sites. This is a big improvement compared to a decade ago.

But HTTPS does not stop you from landing on a fake site, does not protect apps that are badly built, and does not stop a fake hotspot from showing a phishing login page. So HTTPS reduces risk, it does not remove it.

Simple safe habits

  1. Ask staff for the exact network name. Do not just pick the one that sounds right.
  2. Prefer mobile data for banking and UPI. Indian mobile data is affordable, and it is your own connection.
  3. Do not install anything a WiFi page asks for, and never enter your email password to "unlock" WiFi.
  4. Look for the padlock and the correct domain before typing anything sensitive.
  5. Turn off auto connect to open networks and forget the network after use.
  6. Keep sharing off. On laptops, set the network as public, and turn off file and printer sharing.
  7. Keep your device updated so known bugs are fixed.

Do I need a VPN?

A trustworthy VPN encrypts your traffic between your device and the VPN provider, which adds protection on networks you do not trust. CERT-In's 2026 advisory on AI driven risks says to avoid public WiFi for sensitive transactions and use a VPN when necessary.

But a VPN is not magic. It does not stop phishing, malware or scam calls, and a shady free VPN can itself collect your data. Choose a reputable provider, or use your employer's VPN for work. Read our VPN myths post before you install one.

Hotels, trains and stations

  • Hotel WiFi: often shared across many rooms with weak isolation. Use it for streaming and browsing, use mobile data for payments.
  • Railway station and airport WiFi: official free services usually need an OTP on your mobile to log in. That OTP is only for the WiFi login on the official page. If any other page asks for an OTP, stop.
  • Public charging points: use your own charger plugged into a power socket when possible, rather than unknown USB cables or kiosks.

Bonus: secure your home WiFi too

While you are thinking about WiFi, check your home router:

  • Change the default admin password.
  • Use WPA2 or, if available, WPA3 encryption with a long WiFi password. CERT-In's 2026 advisory recommends WPA3 where available.
  • Update the router firmware.
  • Turn off WPS if you do not use it.
  • Create a separate guest network for visitors.

The one line summary

Public WiFi for browsing, mobile data for money. "Free WiFi pe reels dekho, banking nahi." Simple, practical, and it protects you from most risks.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to check email on public WiFi? Major email services use HTTPS, so content is encrypted. The bigger risk is a fake login page. Use the official app, check the domain, and keep two factor authentication on.

What about using my phone as a hotspot for my laptop? Using your own mobile hotspot with a strong password is usually safer than public WiFi, because you control the network.

Is a "5G" or "Free" network name more trustworthy? No. Anyone can choose any network name. Always confirm the exact name with the venue staff.

Key takeaways

  • Public WiFi is okay for low risk browsing, not ideal for banking.
  • Mobile data or your own hotspot is safer for payments.
  • Fake hotspots exist, so confirm the official network name with staff.
  • Turn off auto connect and forget networks after you use them.
